One thing to hold on to as you work

‘Fair’ is not one question, it is three. Fair compared with what — with the Fourteen Points, with what Germany had imposed on Russia at Brest-Litovsk, or with what France actually wanted? Fair to whom — to Germany, to Belgium, to the peoples of eastern Europe? And fair judged when — in 1919, or with the knowledge of 1939?

Answers that score highly are the ones that notice the question splits like this. Answers that score badly treat ‘fair’ as if everybody in 1919 meant the same thing by it.

Task B · Did it make it into the Treaty?

✓ Look back at the pattern. Almost everything aimed at Germany specifically got through. That single observation answers ‘why did the victors not get everything they wanted?’ better than a list of arguments does: the victors agreed on punishing Germany and disagreed on almost everything else, so only the punishment survived.
Focus Task 1.2 · Textbook page 13

None of them went home happy

It is tempting to think the Treaty was what the victors wanted. It was not. Every one of the Big Three was disappointed by it, and two of the three faced hostile publics at home for having conceded too much. Clemenceau lost the French presidency the following year; Wilson could not persuade his own Senate to accept the Treaty at all.

Now commit to an explanation

Focus Task 1.2 asks you to finish this sentence: the victors did not get everything they wanted because… Choose the phrase you find strongest, then argue for it. Part of the mark is for showing you considered the other two.

Your paragraph — state your choice in the first sentence, then support it
Model. The victors did not get everything they wanted mainly because their aims were too different for any single treaty to satisfy all three. Clemenceau wanted Germany crippled, Lloyd George wanted it punished but still able to trade, and Wilson wanted it reformed rather than destroyed. These were not differences of degree but of purpose, so a term that satisfied one leader necessarily disappointed another: the reparations figure that Clemenceau thought too small was one Lloyd George already thought too large. Bullying does not explain the outcome, because Clemenceau lost as often as he won — he was refused the breaking-up of Germany that Poincaré wanted. Public opinion is a better second explanation than bullying, since it is what limited how far each man could compromise, but it operated on all three of them at once and so reinforces the first point rather than replacing it.

That model does what a judgement-first answer should: it names the chosen explanation in sentence one, supports it, then deals with the rival explanations rather than ignoring them. It does not save the judgement for the end.

Task B · The comparison Germany did not make

In March 1918, with Russia collapsing after the Bolshevik Revolution, Germany imposed the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk. Germany was the victor, and it set its own terms. This is the fact that sat behind British and French impatience with German complaints in 1919 — and it is the strongest single piece of ammunition in the argument that Versailles was not unusually harsh.

Brest-Litovsk, March 1918Versailles, June 1919
Imposed byGermany, on RussiaThe Allies, on Germany

Negotiated?No — terms dictated to a defeated RussiaNo — terms presented to Germany on 7 May 1919

Now judge four claims that get made about this comparison. Two of them are the ones weak answers make. Decide, then check.

✓ The move worth learning here is the difference between explaining and excusing. Brest-Litovsk explains why the Allies felt entitled to be harsh, and it undermines the German claim that Versailles was uniquely cruel. It does not by itself prove Versailles was fair, because ‘they would have done worse to us’ is a statement about the Germans, not about the Treaty. An answer that keeps those two apart is doing real evaluation.
Focus Task 1.4 · Textbook pages 16–17

Build the chain from the Treaty to hyperinflation

Almost every candidate can say that the Treaty caused hyperinflation. Very few can say how, and the marks are in the how. A chain of causation means every link is stated: no step is left for the examiner to supply.

Click the eight steps in the order they happened. If you put one in the wrong place, the check will show you where the chain breaks.

Click a step above to begin the chain.
✓ Written as prose, that chain is one strong paragraph: Under the Treaty Germany owed £6,600 million in reparations. It paid the first instalment in 1921 but nothing in 1922, so in January 1923 French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr to take what was owed in goods and raw materials. The German government responded by ordering the workers to strike, which meant there was nothing for the French to take — but also nothing for Germany to sell. With no goods and no income, the government paid the striking workers by printing money, and the money it printed became worthless. Notice how many times the words so, which meant and because appear. If a causation paragraph of yours has none of them, it is description.

Task C · Who actually suffered?

Hyperinflation is usually taught as a national disaster. It was not evenly felt, and the exceptions matter for the Depth Study later.

Ruined
Pensioners and the middle class. Savings that might have bought a house in 1920 would not buy a loaf of bread by 1923. Nothing they had done caused this, and nothing they could do fixed it.
Gained
The government and big industrialists. Large debts were paid off in money that had become worthless. Debt in a collapsing currency is debt that disappears.
Consequence
A group with savings and status lost both, and blamed the Treaty and the politicians who signed it. In 1929, when crisis returned, that group was available to anyone promising to overturn Versailles.

The distinction the textbook makes, and most answers miss

Germans blamed the Treaty for everything that went wrong between 1919 and 1923. The Treaty was genuinely responsible for some of it — the reparations bill, the Ruhr occupation, the territorial and military humiliation. It was not responsible for all of it: the decision to pay strikers by printing money was made in Berlin, and there is a real historical argument that Germany could have afforded to pay and chose not to, in order to force the terms to be revised.

So the strongest sentence you can write about 1919–23 is not ‘the Treaty caused Germany’s problems’. It is that the Treaty caused some of Germany’s problems and was blamed for all of them — and that the blaming, rather than the causing, is what extreme parties were later able to use.

SOURCE 24 · TEXTBOOK PAGE 21
Margaret MacMillan argues that the peacemakers made mistakes, but that they could have done much worse; that they could not foresee the future and certainly could not control it; and that when war came in 1939 it was the result of twenty years of decisions taken or not taken, not of arrangements made in 1919.
Historian writing in Peacemakers, 2001

MacMillan’s argument is the one most students have never met, and it is the most useful to have ready: the gap between 1919 and 1939 is twenty years long, and a great many decisions were taken in it. Treating the Treaty as the sole cause of the Second World War skips all of them.

Step 2 · Your paragraph plan
Model introduction. The Treaty of Versailles was signed in June 1919 after six months of negotiation between three victorious powers with very different aims. Whether it was fair depends on what it is compared with: measured against the Fourteen Points that Germany believed it was surrendering under, it was not, but measured against the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk that Germany itself had imposed on Russia the year before, it was comparatively restrained. Overall the Treaty was harsh but defensible, because although the war guilt clause and the exclusion of Germany from the negotiations were unjustifiable, the territorial and military terms were no more severe than Germany would have imposed had it won.

The sentence in bold does three things at once: it takes a side, it concedes ground, and it gives the reason. An examiner reading only that sentence already knows what the rest of the essay will argue.

Purpose is the one candidates get wrong every session. ‘To inform the public’ earns nothing. Purpose is an action the author wanted: to warn readers that the Treaty would cause another war, to persuade British voters that Germany was being let off, to make Germans angry enough to resist. Always a verb aimed at somebody.

Three fixes that are worth marks on this topic specifically

1. Name terms precisely. Not ‘Germany lost land’ but ‘Alsace-Lorraine to France, the Polish Corridor cutting off East Prussia, the Saar coalfields to France for fifteen years’. Three named terms beat a paragraph of general description.

2. Do not let the Second World War do your work. ‘It caused World War Two’ is an assertion, and the question usually asks about 1919. If you use hindsight, say that you are using it and say who else did — Dyson in 1919, Dawson in 1933.

3. Answer the question that was set. The commonest wasted answer on this topic is a full account of the terms of the Treaty in response to a question about whether it was fair. Everything you know about the terms is only worth marks if it is being used to argue something.
